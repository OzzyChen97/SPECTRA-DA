\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

The experiments ask six questions: (i) how strong are existing label-free
selectors on a shared UGDA candidate bank; (ii) what covariance-aware recovery
can explain under source simulation; (iii) whether that correction transfers
to real targets; (iv) whether registered structural controls repair the
failure; (v) where the remaining regret arises; and (vi) whether any selector
qualifies for final sealed evaluation. All real-target numbers below use four
explicitly open development tasks. The remaining twelve transfers were never
queried.

\subsection{Experimental setup}

\paragraph{Tasks and candidate bank.}
The four pre-registered open-development tasks are
ACMv9$\rightarrow$Citationv1, Citationv1$\rightarrow$ACMv9,
USA$\rightarrow$BRAZIL, and BRAZIL$\rightarrow$USA. Each task contains 675
frozen candidates: five method families (SourceOnly, A2GNN, GRADE, PairAlign,
and ADAlign), three configurations, three seeds, and 15 checkpoints.
Candidate trajectories contain source-validation outputs, unlabeled target
predictions, embeddings, losses, runtime, and memory metadata. Candidate-bank
hashes fix both membership and ordering. The source-simulated study uses the
same candidate schema over 44 leave-one-shift-out development folds.

\paragraph{Selectors.}
We compare source-validation rules, entropy, information maximization, DEV,
SND, Transfer Score, GDE, ALine-S/ALine-D, Agreement Reference, global
committee disagreement, and the proposed \method risk-recovery module
~\citep{you2019dev,saito2021tunerightwayunsupervised,
yang2023transferscore,jiang2021disagreement,baek2022agreement}. We additionally
report the strongest open-development diagnostic pipeline, Agreement
top-$20\%$ followed by Transfer Score reranking. Its provenance is explicit:
Agreement owns the shortlist and Transfer Score owns the final ranking; it is
not a \method variant and is not designated as \emph{ours}.

\paragraph{Metrics and promotion rules.}
For task or fold $t$, normalized regret is
\begin{equation}
  \nregret_t=
  \frac{R_t(h_{\widehat m})-\min_m R_t(h_m)}
  {\max_m R_t(h_m)-\min_m R_t(h_m)+10^{-12}}.
  \label{eq:nregret}
\end{equation}
We report mean and worst normalized regret, selected Micro-F1, rank
correlation, shortlist recall, and decomposition terms. Before Stage-C, we
registered the following promotion requirements: mean regret at most $0.10$,
worst regret at most $0.20$, non-inferiority to Transfer Score on at least
$3/4$ tasks, leave-one-task-out mean regret at most $0.15$, oracle-trajectory
recall at $20\%$ at least $0.75$, source-simulated family-out CVaR degradation
at most $5\%$, runtime below 480 seconds, and zero label-access or protocol
violations.

\subsection{Source-simulated covariance recovery}

Table~\ref{tab:core_ablation} isolates the risk-recovery mechanism over 44
source-simulated folds. The pre-registered full configuration reaches mean
normalized regret $0.01415$, $\cvar_{20\%}=0.04793$, worst-fold regret
$0.08388$, and a $95.45\%$ top-$5\%$ hit rate. Removing covariance increases
mean regret to $0.05057$; the paired mean-difference bootstrap interval is
$[0.01583,0.06026]$. Covariance correction is therefore the dominant
empirical component when its calibration data match the evaluation regime.

\subsection{Simulation-to-real transport failure}

The source-simulated gain does not survive the real-shift information
boundary. A low-rank risk-correction transport variant reaches family-out mean
regret $0.06977$, while descriptor distance has Spearman correlation
$-0.04954$ with risk-correction distance. On real targets, \method calibration
reaches $0.32006$ mean regret. A task-wise oracle over fixed covariance
shrinkage strengths indicates that different tasks prefer different
corrections, but label-free pair consistency selects systematically excessive
correction and reaches $0.339996$. These results localize the failure to
deciding \emph{when} a source-simulated covariance correction is trustworthy,
not to solving the pair-sum recovery system once the correction is known.

\subsection{Pre-registered Stage A/B/C no-go audit}

We allowed only bounded, hypothesis-specific follow-ups. Stage A tested
covariance trust and fixed shrinkage; Stage B tested selector consensus; Stage
C tested cross-fitting, trajectory evidence, coverage floors, fixed auxiliary
signals, and bootstrap stability. Table~\ref{tab:structural_audit} reports the
strongest or decisive control from each family. Every row preserves the same
candidate bank and reports zero label accesses and protocol violations.

The negative controls are structurally informative. Cross-fitting does not
remove a harmful self-vote effect; requiring repeated checkpoint evidence
worsens selection; coverage floors cannot help a shortlist that already
contains the relevant trajectories; and stability does not identify the
better expert. The parameter-free stability router selects the non-inferior
expert on only $2/4$ open-development tasks and therefore fails its registered
$3/4$ qualification.

\subsection{Shortlist-specific error decomposition}

The final failure analysis distinguishes whether the shortlist misses the
right region or the reranker selects poorly within that region. Let $S$ be the
Agreement shortlist. We expand trajectories represented in $S$ only for
analysis and decompose the selected regret into method coverage, trajectory
coverage, checkpoint coverage, and within-shortlist reranking. Table
~\ref{tab:shortlist_decomposition} shows that the shortlist represents the
global-oracle method and trajectory on all four tasks. The remaining error is
therefore concentrated after coverage has already succeeded.

USA$\rightarrow$BRAZIL makes the diagnosis especially clear: the global-oracle
candidate is already in the shortlist, so its entire $0.223881$ regret is
created by Transfer Score reranking. This explains why trajectory expansion,
coverage floors, and method balancing fail. The unresolved problem is
top-of-ranking calibration among shortlisted checkpoints, not finding more
trajectories.

The authoritative decision is
\texttt{stop\_no\_freeze\_no\_sealed\_evaluation}. No selector is frozen as
\emph{ours}, and the final twelve target-label files remain unqueried. This
decision is part of the experimental result: the benchmark demonstrates not
only how to compare selectors, but also how to stop development when apparent
mean gains do not satisfy robustness and cross-task criteria.
