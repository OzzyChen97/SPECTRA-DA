\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Unsupervised graph domain adaptation (UGDA) transfers a node classifier from a
labeled source graph to an unlabeled target graph whose attributes and topology
may follow a different distribution~\citep{wu2020unsupervised,dai2022graph,liu2023structural}.
Recent methods address propagation mismatch, conditional structure shift,
homophily shift, and composite feature--structure shift with increasingly
specialized adaptation objectives~\citep{liu2023structural,liu2024rethinking,liu2024pairwisealignmentimprovesgraph,fang2025attribute, yang2025disentangled,pmlr-v267-fang25g,chen2026learningadaptivedistributionalignment}.
These advances answer how a candidate model should be trained, but leave a
separate deployment question unresolved: without any target label, which
algorithm, hyperparameter setting, random seed, and checkpoint should be used?

This question is obscured when target labels are used after training to select
the best checkpoint. For example, our audit of the public ADAlign
implementation\footnote{\url{https://github.com/gxingyu/ADAlign}, audited on
August 20, 2026.} finds that the training loop computes target Micro- and
Macro-F1 at every epoch and reports the maximum target score. This procedure
does not leak target labels into gradient updates, but it does implement a
\emph{target-label oracle selector}; consequently, the reported model is not
available in the intended unlabeled deployment setting. Existing UGDA
benchmarks standardize training and transfer tasks~\citep{liu2024revisitingbenchmarkingunderstandingunsupervised},
whereas general-domain label-free selectors based on embedded validation,
neighborhood density, transfer scores, or cross-model agreement do not
explicitly model target graph structure~\citep{you2019dev,saito2021tunerightwayunsupervised,
yang2023transferscore,jiang2021disagreement,baek2022agreement}.

Target-label-free UGDA selection is technically difficult because observable
model agreement is not individual model accuracy. Given candidate errors
$E_m$ and $E_n$, their pairwise disagreement contains the sum of individual
risks minus twice their error covariance. Classical unsupervised classifier
evaluation therefore relies on restrictive structure in cross-classifier
errors~\citep{parisi2014ranking,pmlr-v38-jaffe15}. The difficulty is amplified in trajectory
banks: adjacent checkpoints and related GNN backbones share errors, while graph
shifts change which shared errors appear at local, community, and global
scales. A useful selector must exploit graph structure without assuming that
candidate errors are independent.

We propose \method, a covariance-aware model-selection layer for a frozen UGDA
candidate bank.
As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, \method first decomposes hard or
soft candidate predictions using a tight spectral frame on the unlabeled
target graph. It then converts band-wise pair disagreement into individual
risk through a covariance-corrected pair-sum system. Because target error
covariance is unobservable, the method estimates it from labeled
source-simulated feature, topology, homophily, label-prior, and conditional
structure shifts, matched to the target with unlabeled graph and committee
descriptors. Nonnegative recovery produces the label-free selection score;
the robust refit and source-only bootstrap term are implementation refinements
rather than core contributions.

The current evidence supports three contributions, with a narrower scope than
a state-of-the-art selector claim. First, we formalize \benchmark, a
sealed-label protocol that jointly evaluates algorithm, hyperparameter, seed,
and checkpoint selection over immutable candidate trajectories. Second, we
derive covariance-aware risk recovery from pairwise disagreement: the tight
spectral frame gives an exact error decomposition, the pair-sum system exposes
cross-model error covariance as the hidden term, and the recovery bound shows
how covariance misspecification propagates to risk error. Third, we identify
calibration reliability as the central empirical bottleneck. On four
source-simulated development tasks and 44 held-out shifts, covariance
correction reduces mean normalized regret from $0.02278$ to $0.01415$, while
spectral conditioning adds only a modest and statistically inconclusive
increment over global covariance. On the separately sealed real-target
development comparison, Transfer Score attains lower top-1 regret ($0.1467$
versus $0.2560$), whereas our selector attains higher Kendall correlation.
This split shows that source-simulated calibration can succeed while real-shift
top-1 selection still fails; consequently, the paper studies the reliability
of covariance correction rather than claiming full 16-transfer state of the
art.
